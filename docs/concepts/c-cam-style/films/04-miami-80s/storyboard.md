# A Day in the Life of a Miami Gangster, 1980s — storyboard (62 s, English captions, no audio)

Working poster title: **"Would You Survive as a Miami Gangster in the 80s?"** (second person, like films 1–3).

TONE: satire, no glorification. The joke is the gap between the TV myth (pastel suits, speedboats, neon) and the real
day: a shabby hotel full of retirees, a borrowed car that will not start, the bus in August, a boss who humiliates
you, cash you cannot even bank, paranoia, a friend wearing a wire, an escape that runs dry, and a courtroom. No
how-to: crime is never shown or explained (no drugs on screen, no dealing, no method), no violence, no weapons, no
gore. Fictional cast only (no real people, gangs, cartels, brands or logos; the car and boat are generic shapes).

## FACTS (only what we can stand behind; qualitative wording, no invented numbers)

| On screen | Fact | Why we can say it |
|---|---|---|
| "Miami, the mid-1980s." / Miami's reputation | In the early 1980s Miami was internationally associated with the cocaine trade and the crime around it. | Widely documented (e.g. the 1981 *Time* cover story "Paradise Lost?"); kept qualitative, no statistics on screen. |
| "On TV, it's pastel suits, speedboats and neon." | *Miami Vice* premiered on NBC in September 1984 and its pastel, neon, speedboat look shaped the popular image of 80s Miami. | Well-established TV history. The show is not named on screen and nothing from it is copied: the TV picture is our own generic sunset + speedboat. |
| "Real life: a shabby South Beach hotel. Your neighbours are retirees." | Before its late-80s/90s revival, South Beach's Art Deco hotels were largely run-down and home to many elderly retirees on fixed incomes. | Well documented (the Miami Design Preservation League was founded in 1976 and the Art Deco district was listed on the National Register in 1979 because the buildings were threatened). |
| "Banks have to report big cash deposits." | US banks must file a report for large cash transactions (the Currency Transaction Report under the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970). | Law in force throughout the 1980s. The threshold is deliberately not on screen (not needed for the joke, and no how-to). |
| "Since 1982, a federal task force has been hunting people like you." | The South Florida Task Force, a federal anti-drug effort chaired by Vice President George H. W. Bush, was created in 1982. | Well-established; the real people are not named or shown. The Agent is a fictional comedy foil. |
| "The glamour ends in a courtroom." / "It never ends well." | Prosecutions of drug-trade figures in South Florida rose in the 1980s; informants and recorded conversations were common tools. | Qualitative; supports the moral, not a statistic. |

Fiction, clearly comedic (not presented as history): the borrowed car, the bus, the boss's hour, the cash in a freezer
and the oven mitts, the specific wire, the speedboat running dry, the agent's sandwich. The Mariel boatlift (1980) was
considered and left out: it is a sensitive refugee story and the film's short running time could only have used it
as a cheap backdrop for crime, which would be unfair to the people who came.

## CAST (original characters, each individually hand-built)

- **YOU** (`cast/you.js`) — a small-time gofer who watched too much TV: short and soft under huge shoulder pads,
  feathered mullet, sunburnt snub nose (peels by the end), wispy moustache, worried eyebrows, aviators worn at the
  wrong moments. White linen suit over a dusty-pink T-shirt, a fake gold chain. Second outfit: vest + striped boxers.
- **THE BOSS** (`cast/boss.js`) — a low wide block in a teal palm-print shirt, gold chain (the one real luxury),
  amber-tint aviators, slicked hair with grey wings, jowls, an unlit cigar. Yells; spit flies.
- **THE ACCOUNTANT** (`cast/accountant.js`) — your best friend: a pear on skinny legs, perm cloud, droopy moustache,
  low rimless glasses, pocket protector, sweat rings, sandals with socks. Later: oven mitts, fogged glasses, the wire.
- **THE AGENT** (`cast/agent.js`) — the task force in one tired, very patient man: a tall plank, flat-top, grey
  short-sleeved shirt, narrow tie. Recurring gag: he is in nearly every shot, behind a newspaper with eye holes or
  eating a sandwich, never hurrying.
- Background (`cast/crowd.js`): retirees (curlers, knee socks, a hand fan, knitting), a man in a loud shirt, the
  judge, the bailiff.

sedan → rowboat → courtroom is the running gag; the Boss's cigar is never lit, from the poster to the cell.
